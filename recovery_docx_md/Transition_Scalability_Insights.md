Once you combine **distributed modular structure** with the **New World
Web as a shared coordination layer**, you cannot leave failure handling
implicit. The stronger the coordination system, the more important it
becomes to define, in advance, how it prevents misuse, degrades safely,
and recovers without reintroducing hierarchy. Research on commons
governance, high-reliability organizations, supply-chain dynamics, and
distributed infrastructure all point the same way: durable systems are
not the ones that assume smooth operation, but the ones that monitor
continuously, correct early, and use layered fallback modes. 

The practical implication for your model is this:

**For every failure mode, you now want three linked layers**

1.  **Prevention** --- what makes the failure less likely.

2.  **Detection and containment** --- what stops it from cascading.

3.  **Recovery and redesign** --- what restores function and closes the
    structural hole.

That architecture is strongly supportable. Ostrom-style commons research
emphasizes monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict-resolution
mechanisms, and nested governance as recurring ingredients of durable
self-governance. High-reliability research emphasizes preoccupation with
failure, reluctance to simplify, and deference to expertise during
incidents. Supply-chain research shows that distorted demand signals
amplify upstream unless information is shared and delays are controlled.
Distributed energy and microgrid research shows that modularity,
islanding, and fallback operation improve resilience when parts of the
system fail. 

So the upgraded principle for the Transition model becomes:

**Scalability requires not just coordination, but failure-aware
coordination.**

More concretely, the combined architecture should work like this:

A **local node** handles most ordinary decisions.

A **regional layer** helps balance shortages, surpluses, and
interdependencies.

The **New World Web** provides visibility, signaling, auditability, and
matching.

A **failure protocol layer** determines what happens when any of those
malfunction.

That means each identified failure mode should now be rewritten in a
more disciplined form:

**Failure mode**

What goes wrong.

**Why it emerges in a modular + NWW system**

The actual pathway by which the architecture creates or amplifies the
risk.

**Prevention mechanisms**

Structural constraints, transparency rules, role separation, buffers,
quotas, redundancy, nested authority, algorithm auditability, and so on.

**Detection indicators**

The measurable warning signs.

**Containment actions**

What gets frozen, localized, downgraded, or rerouted.

**Recovery path**

How the system returns to normal without handing permanent power to a
central emergency authority.

**Redesign rule**

What structural change is required so the same failure mode does not
simply recur.

That last part matters. Systems often fail because they correct the
event but not the mechanism.

A few examples will show how this should look.

For **demand distortion**, prevention should rely on multi-signal
validation, because supply-chain research is clear that distorted or
delayed signals create bullwhip-like amplification. Detection would
watch for request spikes, request-to-usage mismatch, and anomalous
regional divergence. Containment would freeze auto-escalation for the
affected item class and switch temporarily to conservative allocation.
Recovery would reconcile observed usage against requests and then retune
forecast weights. 

For **governance capture**, prevention should come from nested
governance, transparent decision logs, role rotation, appeal paths, and
challenge rights. That is very close to what commons research repeatedly
finds in durable systems. Detection would watch for concentration of
authority, declining reversal rates on appeal, and repeated routing of
exceptions through the same actors. Containment would suspend
discretionary authority in the affected domain and open the decision
trail for review. Recovery would redistribute authority and revise the
procedure that allowed power concentration to accumulate. 

For **New World Web data-layer failure**, prevention should include
redundancy, distributed control, local caches, manual fallback
procedures, and careful separation between public coordination data and
sensitive data. Distributed-infrastructure research and microgrid work
both support the value of modular operation and fallback modes rather
than total dependence on a single central layer. Detection would focus
on integrity mismatches, unexplained state divergence, and
synchronization failures. Containment would downgrade from automated to
supervised mode and allow local nodes to operate temporarily in
semi-islanded fashion. Recovery would reconcile state across nodes and
only then restore full shared coordination. 

For **over-centralization through the NWW itself**, prevention has to be
explicit: the system must distinguish between **visibility** and
**command**. A coordination layer that sees everything can easily drift
into a layer that rules everything. That is where polycentric theory
matters. The web should enable federated awareness and matching, not
default central override. Detection would watch for growing numbers of
decisions migrating upward without necessity, or algorithms quietly
becoming the de facto governors. Containment would require rule-based
re-localization of decisions. Recovery would include redesign of the
interface, authority boundaries, and algorithm governance. That follows
directly from the literature favoring nested and polycentric
arrangements over single-center control in complex commons settings.
